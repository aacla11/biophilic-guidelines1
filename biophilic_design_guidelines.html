Biophilic design is grounded in the science of how humans relate to the natural world. These foundational principles, drawn from the Kellert et al. (2008) framework, organize every strategy in this guidebook.

Category 01 · Direct Experience of Nature

Contact with living systems and natural forces

Direct encounters with nature — natural light, air movement, water, vegetation, animals, and weather — provide the most powerful biophilic stimulus. In residential design, this category encompasses daylighting strategies, operable windows, indoor plants, living walls, water features, and connections to outdoor gardens and landscapes. Research consistently links direct nature contact to reduced stress, improved mood, and faster physiological recovery.

Category 02 · Indirect Experience of Nature

Representation of nature in materials, form, and pattern

Indirect biophilic experience occurs through the use of natural materials, organic patterns, imagery of nature, and age/change in materials. Wood, stone, rattan, linen, and clay — and design patterns that echo natural geometries (fractals, spirals, flowing curves) — all activate biophilic responses even in the absence of living systems. This category is often the most cost-accessible entry point for residential biophilic design.

Category 03 · Experience of Space and Place

Spatial configurations that echo natural environments

Humans evolved with preferences for certain spatial configurations — the ability to see without being seen (prospect and refuge), variable ceiling heights, layered spaces, and mystery (the invitation to explore further). These spatial qualities can be designed into residential environments through varied ceiling heights, alcoves, semi-enclosed niches, thresholds, and the careful orchestration of views. They are architectural moves, not material additions, and carry no additional construction cost when integrated at the design stage.

Evidence base — selected findings

15% reduction in reported stress with daily nature views (Ulrich, 1984)
26% improvement in cognitive function in daylit vs. artificially lit spaces (Yin et al., 2018)
30% average energy savings from passive daylighting strategies (DOE, 2023)
